# Choosing a first customer segment

Sample note written for this repository. Original text, generic advice.

A new company cannot sell to everyone who might benefit. Picking one segment first makes every later task easier: the message, the list, the product scope, and the references you can offer.

## What makes a good first segment

- The problem is frequent. Monthly pain is easier to sell against than yearly pain.
- One person can say yes. If a purchase needs four departments to agree, the sales cycle will outlast your cash.
- Buyers know each other. A segment where people attend the same events and read the same trade news spreads word of mouth.
- You can reach them. If you cannot build a list of one hundred named people, the segment is too vague.

## Scoring candidates

List three or four candidate segments. For each, score from one to five on pain frequency, ease of reaching the buyer, length of sales cycle, and ability to pay. Add a note on evidence. A score backed by five interviews is worth more than a score backed by a guess.

## Commit for a fixed period

Choose one segment and work only on it for eight to twelve weeks. Set a simple target, such as ten qualified conversations and two pilots. Review at the end. Switching segments every two weeks produces no usable signal.

## Signs you picked wrong

- People take the meeting but nobody moves to a pilot.
- Every prospect asks for a different product.
- The buyer likes it and has no budget authority.

## Expanding later

Move to a neighbouring segment once the first one yields repeatable sales and a few buyers willing to act as references. Neighbouring means a similar buyer with a similar problem.
